# Non-Human Biologics in the Stratosphere: A Study by Aerostratospheric

> Research Program v1.0 (October 2026). DRAFT pending public-copy review before release. Printable PDF: `non-human-biologics-in-the-stratosphere.pdf` (planned public URL /docs/non-human-biologics-in-the-stratosphere.pdf).

By the Aerostratospheric Team · Research program of xBioNexus, the Aerostratospheric Advanced Environmental Research Base · Program version 1.0, October 2026

**Status:** This is a planned research program. Ground-control work with our non-human biologics has begun. No non-human biologics have flown yet, and no results are claimed. Aerostratospheric has flown its first data mission, which did not carry biologics. Plans and timelines may change.

## Program summary

*Non-Human Biologics in the Stratosphere* is an open, repeatable study of how small, harmless non-human biologics (tardigrades, the microscopic animals also called water bears) respond to a real high-altitude balloon flight. We will fly sealed Petri dishes of active animals to the stratosphere, compare them with matched dishes that stay on the ground, and publish every data sheet, microscope video clip and payload log openly as the work happens.

The program is run by an experienced STEM team at Aerostratospheric, an Illinois nonprofit corporation, from our Corporate Office in Olney, Illinois, with ground environmental data from our Fixed Ground Weather Station in Casey, Illinois and, as it comes online, our xBioNexus Launch Site and Environmental Research Base in Birds, Illinois.

## Objectives

- **Survival in the active state.** Find out whether non-human biologics carried in their active, hydrated state survive a real stratospheric flight. Much earlier work flew animals dried into a dormant "tun" state, so data on active animals is less common.
- **Revival time.** Measure how long flown animals take to become active again after recovery, and look at that alongside the UV, cold, low pressure and radiation recorded by the payload.
- **After-effects.** Track egg-laying, hatching and survival over the weeks after flight, compared with ground controls.
- **Repeatability.** Check whether the two flight dishes agree with each other, and whether results hold across repeat flights using the same design.
- **Open science and education.** Build a public, reusable dataset that students, teachers and researchers can follow and use.

## Research questions and hypotheses

We state our hypotheses in advance so the results can be judged fairly. These are questions to test, not expected findings.

- **H1 (survival):** The share of animals alive after recovery in the flight dishes (C1, C2) will differ from the share in their paired ground controls (B1, B2). The null hypothesis is no difference.
- **H2 (revival):** Time to revival after recovery will be related to the payload-recorded exposure (minimum temperature, minimum pressure, UV and radiation).
- **H3 (reproduction):** Egg-laying, hatching and juvenile survival over the following weeks will differ between flown dishes and their paired controls.
- **H4 (agreement):** The two flight-and-control pairs will show the same direction of effect, if there is one.

## Study design

**Organism and culture.** *Hypsibius exemplaris* (strain Z151), culture and lot 31585554, received October 28, 2026 from Carolina Biological Supply Company. Carolina is an educational supplier and vendor only, not a partner or sponsor.

**Groups.**

- **AERO-554-A, home habitat.** One large, shallow tank that never leaves the lab. It is the stock and backup population that supplies the test dishes. It is not the formal control.
- **AERO-554-B1 and B2, ground controls.** Petri dishes that follow the launch group through every step on the ground, but do not fly.
- **AERO-554-C1 and C2, flight dishes.** Petri dishes that fly to the stratosphere in the sealed payload.

**Fixed pairing.** C1 is paired with B1, and C2 is paired with B2. Each pair is set up on the same day from the same stock, with the same spring water, water depth, feeding and starting count, so the main difference between them is the flight.

**Husbandry.** Room-temperature spring water kept 0.5 to 1 cm deep; small pieces of algae disk as food; partial water changes; leftover food removed. Dishes are labeled by group and date.

**Flight.** A high-altitude balloon will carry a small sealed payload with the flight dishes and sensors logging temperature, pressure, altitude, UV and radiation. The payload descends by parachute, is tracked and recovered, and the dishes are returned for observation.

## Data collection

All records follow our standard operating procedure, SOP AERO-XBN-SOP-2026-001, using three documents:

- **Form F1, Ground Culture Log Sheet:** routine checks of A, B1, B2 (and C1, C2 before and after flight).
- **Form F2, Flight Sample Record:** one per flight dish, with its paired control recorded at the same times.
- **Guide G1, Weekly Research Documentation Guide:** how each check is done and recorded.

**Every 2 to 3 days:** date and time (Central time), researcher, room and water temperature, water clarity, water change, feeding, and separate counts of active, sluggish, tun, dead and eggs. Behavior is recorded with fixed observation codes (paired, clustered, feeding, molting, still, cloudy water) instead of free-text notes.

**Weekly:** a full count of each dish on the same day and time, plus a 10 to 30 second microscope video clip of each dish at the same magnification and lighting, named with a fixed pattern and logged by clip number.

**Flight weeks:** paired C and B counts before launch; payload readings (maximum altitude, minimum pressure, minimum and maximum temperature, UV, radiation) with the logger file name; recovery time and time to revival for each flight dish; then paired counts at recovery, 24 hours, 72 hours and 1 week, followed by reproduction checks over the next weeks.

**Ground environment:** each flight will be paired with ground weather data from our Fixed Ground Weather Station and, once installed, solar, UV index and radiation readings from xBioNexus.

## Planned analysis

- Survival as the share of animals alive (active plus sluggish plus tun) out of the starting count, compared within each pair (C1 vs B1, C2 vs B2) at each time point.
- Time to revival for flight dishes, plotted against payload exposure values.
- Eggs, hatching and juvenile counts over time, compared between paired dishes.
- Agreement between the two pairs, and later across flights. With small numbers, results will be reported as counts, percentages and simple comparisons, with confidence intervals where they make sense, and pooled across repeat flights as the dataset grows.
- All raw sheets, clips and logs will be published, so anyone can check or redo the analysis.

## What makes this program different

- **Open data, published as we go** through the Aerostratospheric Data Hub: scanned sheets, microscope clips and payload logs, not just a final summary years later.
- **Repeat flights with the same design,** building a consistent dataset over time.
- **Paired ground environmental data** from our own stations for every flight.
- **Microscope video of every weekly count,** easy to check, share and teach with.
- **A rural, community research model** that schools and the public in southern Illinois and beyond can follow and take part in.

## Limitations

- Two flight dishes and two controls per flight is a small sample. One flight can show a direction, not a firm conclusion; repeat flights are needed.
- Flight conditions (temperature, UV, radiation, pressure, landing) vary from flight to flight and are not fully controlled.
- Ground controls share handling but not the cold, low pressure or UV of flight, so the study cannot separate each factor on its own.
- Counting by microscope involves some human judgment. We reduce this with a fixed method, the same magnification and video records.
- Results apply to this strain under these conditions and should not be generalized beyond that.
